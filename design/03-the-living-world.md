# The living world

Draft 1, 2026-09-13. What is out there, what it does while you are not looking, and what happens
when you interfere. Everything named here is built and playable unless marked otherwise. Read
[02-settlements-and-work.md](02-settlements-and-work.md) first.

## The pitch

**The island was full before you arrived, and it runs on the same machinery you do.**

A town's baker is a person with the baking skill who walks to a mill, carries flour to an oven, and
bakes. The bread on the shop's shelf is bread he made. The shopkeeper's money is coins in his
pocket, and if he dies somebody else in that town takes over the shop.

There is no simulation layer behind the towns. They are colonies, played by the game instead of
by you.

## Towns are records, not buildings

A settlement is a **roster of people with stable identities**, and that roster outlives the
physical town. A resident is a record first and a body second.

**Roles are filled by succession.** Kill a town's trader
and the shop does not close forever. After a while, somebody else in that town is the trader and
it reopens. Roles are things like trader, guard, tavern keeper. A town that loses too many people
without replacement has a problem it has to solve by other means.

**Towns can be poor.** A settlement whose larder is empty is in poverty: its people are hungry
and its shop is thin, because the food was not produced.

## Trade

Money is **coins, physically**. The trader's purse is their inventory. The shop's stock is a real
chest standing in the world.

You walk a person up to a trader, a basket opens, prices come off the item's own worth plus the
trader's margin, and both sides have to actually have what they are offering. A trader with an
empty purse cannot buy your loot no matter how much it is worth.

And because the stock is a chest, **you can rob it**. Robbing a town empties that shop until the
town makes more.

## Travel

The island has traffic. Caravans move between towns carrying **a cart of real goods**, which they
sell into a shop through the same trade the player uses. Wanderers walk. Immigrants arrive and
settle, which is how a town fills a role it lost.

Visitors need somewhere to sleep and rent a bed from a tavern keeper, or roll out a bedroll if
there is none.

Long journeys follow **roads**, node to node, not a straight line through the wilderness.

## Territory

Territory is derived from what is actually there. Enough finished buildings close together are
recognised as a settlement, and that settlement claims ground around itself, which **grows as the
place grows**.

Inside somebody else's territory you cannot build. The map is carved by who has put up buildings
and kept them, not by designed zones. A campfire and a bedroll
are the exceptions, so a traveller can always make camp.

## Reputation and crime

Every faction has a standing relationship with every other, including yours, and it moves.

**Being seen is the mechanic.** Theft, trespass, lockpicking and murder all check whether anybody
could see you do it: real line of sight, real light, real distance. An
unwitnessed crime costs you nothing. A witnessed one costs you a town's goodwill and can raise an
alarm, which locks doors and sends everyone who is not a guard home.

Guards patrol visible routes, investigate things that look wrong, and notice better than ordinary
people. Darkness helps you; torches and lamps make you easier to see.

Locks are real. Chests and doors can be locked, and picking one takes time, during which somebody
might walk past.

## Raids

Bands arrive on a schedule, march toward you in formation, and gather at your gate before
committing. They can **parley** rather than attack. If they win, they loot a chest and leave; if
they lose badly enough, they break and run. Downed raiders lie where they fell, like anyone else.

A raid is ten to twenty people. Storming a fortified town with its militia turned out is about
forty, and that is the ceiling for any fight in this game.

## Time

Real time, pausable, at three speeds. A day/night cycle and a calendar that everything else hangs
off. Crops grow on it, wounds heal on it, schedules run on it, and raids are scheduled against it.

Night changes what can be seen, when gates close, who is asleep, and who is out.

## What this means if you are writing lore

The systems above are fixed. The fiction over them is not; the world bible is one evening of
brainstorming, see [../lore/](../lore/). But fiction has to live in the machinery, and a few things
it cannot do:

* **No empires, no armies, no kings with legions.** The largest thing that exists is a town of a
  few hundred, and the largest force is a band.
* **No abstract wealth.** A rich faction is one with full chests and a lot of coins in a lot of
  pockets. It cannot have a treasury that is a number.
* **No teleporting, no instant messages, no magic logistics.** Everything that moves is carried by
  somebody along a road.
* **No faction that only exists offscreen.** If a people is in the world, its towns are simulated
  towns full of people doing jobs. A faction that could not be a town is a faction that cannot be
  built.
* **Anything a townsperson does, a player's person can do too,** and vice versa. There is one set
  of rules.

Ideas that need new machinery are not banned, they cost time. Say which kind you are proposing.

## Open

* **Names for everything.** No faction, people, town or island in the game has a real name yet, and
  neither does the game.
* **What happens after a parley.** The hook exists and nothing listens to it: a band that comes to
  talk currently waits and then leaves.
* **Diplomacy in general.** Reputation moves through actions only. There is no way to negotiate,
  ally, pay tribute or declare anything.
* **The player's own town as an identity** rather than a cluster of buildings with a claim.
* **Quests, contracts, or any directed content.** None exist, and it is not decided whether any
  should.
* **Balance, entirely.**
