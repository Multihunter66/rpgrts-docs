# Lore direction sheet

2026-09-15. The one page handed to anyone who writes, draws or designs for this world. It says where the world is going and what is fixed, and it leaves the rest to the people doing the work. **What is fixed is the aesthetic, the tone, the frame and a handful of mysteries. Who actually lives on the island is not decided yet, and designing that is the job.** The companion is `2026-09-15-concept-art-direction-sheet.md`, which covers how it looks. The bible files in `docs/lore/` fix the frame and the aesthetic and leave the peoples open. Names in `[brackets]` are placeholders; the island and the game have no name yet.

---

## 1. Where we want to go

**A frontier Western with post-apocalypse grit, on an island built on the ruins of a civilisation nobody remembers.** Steam and pneumatics on the common tier, crude retro-futurist high tech above it, dust masks against the storms, a desert under a hard sun, and monumental stone and pale alloy under the sand that nobody living made. Kenshi's indifference, Fallout's readable rubble, Metro's hostile air, a Western's posture.

That is the destination. Everything below is either a rule that protects it or a space left open for you.

## 2. The tone in one line

**The world does not care about you, and it is not trying to kill you either.** Things are hard because they are hard, not because a villain made them so.

## 3. The frame: what is fixed

These are the load-bearing facts. Build on them; do not contradict them.

- **An island, about 100 km across.** Desert coast, dry terraced mountains inside. Green only where people make it with water. Water is the resource. Timber is wealth.
- **Something built the ruins and is gone.** Monumental stone cut to a tolerance no chisel explains, a paved road network across the whole island with way-stations a day apart, machines still walking and working, no writing anyone can read, and a scale that almost fits: doorways a little too tall, stairs a little too steep. The settlers say "the Builders". Every town sits where a Builder road happened to be.
- **Humans arrived a few generations ago and do not remember how.** Founding stories contradict each other. No document from before, no known home, nobody serious talks about leaving. They settled the coast, built towns of cast stone and riveted iron, and the coast is now full.
- **The humans are not alone, and they were not first.** The interior is somebody else's country. Whoever is up there has been there far longer, knows the roads and the machines better, and has noticed the surveys coming up the old roads. The first war the player sees is the coast pushing uphill against them.
- **The machines are terrain with rules, not an enemy faction.** They have jobs nobody understands, they are not one kind, they fight each other in certain places, they collect worked metal, and most of them ignore most people most of the time.
- **Nobody is good.** Every faction is useful and dangerous. The uphill push is a land grab by people who need land against people who were there first; the game shows it plainly and takes no side.
- **Scale is human.** The biggest thing on the island is a town of a few hundred and a militia of fifty. No empires, no capital, no armies of thousands.
- **The tech ladder** (§6) and **the six mysteries** (§8).

## 4. What is open: the peoples

**We do not know who is on the island yet.** The bible fixes that someone lives in the highlands and was there first, plus the machines and the outlaws, and nothing else about the peoples. An earlier draft with two named alien species was withdrawn on 2026-09-15 so that nothing reads as canon. **Designing the peoples, from how many there are to what they want, is the writer's job.** What a proposal has to respect:

- **Humanoid, on a shared skeleton.** Every species is two arms, two legs, a head, in one proportion family, so it shares the animation library and the armour sockets. Difference lives in the head, the skin, the hands, the build, the bearing and the culture, never in the limb count. An exotic body plan is a later, separate cost. (See the art sheet for why this is realistic-proportion, not stylised.)
- **Not primitive, not evil, not noble savages.** A people that was here first has institutions, a law, a technology that is different rather than worse, and reasons of its own. A stranded people is nobody's responsibility and everybody's go-between. Whatever you design, it wants something concrete and close to the ground.
- **Each species is good at something the others are not**, in a way that would show up in a skills table.
- **Their relation to the Builders is a mystery, not a fact.** A species may claim descent. The claim is never confirmed or denied by anything the game states.
- **Their relation to the machines is practical.** Someone knows how to walk past them. Nobody explains how.
- **They fit the aesthetic**: the highlands are precise fitted stone, terraces, textiles, ceramics, bronze, composure; the roads and the coast are the settlers' assembled iron and brass. A people's things should be recognisable from thirty metres and should look *finished* where the settlers' look *assembled*.

The number of species, their names, their bodies within the humanoid rule, their societies, their histories since the Silence, their competences, and how they treat humans and each other are all yours to propose. So is whether a "waking machine" that thinks for itself exists.

## 5. The settlers: one draft, mostly decided

The humans are the most built part, because the game's machinery (towns, trade, work, raids) already runs on them. Three institutions cross town lines: the **`[Guild]`** (merchants, caravans, prices, debt; owns a piece of every town, pays for several militias), the **`[Militias]`** (one per town of size, the closest thing to law, some honest and some a mayor's private army), and the **`[Technicians]`** (the youngest: the people who took Builder tech apart and now build the coast's only high tech, sell to both sides, and are the reason nobody has marched uphill yet, because nobody wants to go without their machines). Towns have characters: fishing town, mill town, mine town, Guild town, a warlord's town. Between them, on the roads: caravans, nomads, outlaws, and the salvagers who go into ruins on purpose.

Their names, their politics in detail, and the Technicians' halls and refusals are open. The shape is not.

## 6. Technology: the ladder

Slow, handmade, mechanical. The coast builds precise machines one at a time by hand and cannot mass-produce anything. Every machine shows how it works.

| Tier | Name | Who | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Hand craft | Anyone with a bench | Knives, bows, iron swords, leather, palisades, campfires |
| 1 | Frontier machines | Towns with a smithy and a millwright | Steam generators, wind turbines, mills, pneumatic rifles, iron plate armour, stone walls, cables and lamps |
| 2 | Master work and relics | A handful of craftsmen; the ruins | Laser muskets built around a salvaged Builder cell, repeater rifles (a few dozen on the island), precision optics, salvage limbs |
| 3 | Settler high tech | The Technicians, and a player who has done the work | Cell generators, coil rifles and arc guns, sensors, radio, powered prosthetics, powered armour, the first engines on wheels |
| 4 | Builder tech | Nobody | Drones, mechs, whatever powers the ruins. Salvage only |

**The player can build everything but tier 4**, at a cost. A fast weapon is an event; when one appears, the world should notice. Any other people's ladder is different, not worse.

## 7. The island

Think of the Andes compressed onto an island and take it literally: **the coast is desert.** Coast and lowlands are the settlers' (towns at river mouths and Builder cisterns, fishing, harbours, irrigated wheat). The foothills are the frontier (half-built towns, sites recently abandoned or recently fortified, way-stations held by whoever got there first, the good iron). The highlands are the other people's country (terraces, stone towns, ruins still lived in, and the great Builder cities on the ridges that even they do not enter). The dead zones are sites, not a region: machines dense and hostile, ruins richest, nobody lives there. The sea is the one thing that lives without being made to; boats that go too far do not return.

**The roads are the Builders'** and indifferent to every border drawn since. The iron gradient (poor coastal iron, good foothill seams) drives the frontier war; the water gradient drives everything else. The island's outline, its animals, its climate and seasons are open.

## 8. What the game never says

Six load-bearing mysteries. No text, data row, faction blurb or NPC line resolves them; every in-game source that touches them disagrees with some other source, and each account must read as a plausible distortion of the same real thing, not an invention.

1. What the Builders were: human, not human, something that made humans. All three are believed by somebody.
2. What ended them. The ruins look abandoned, not destroyed.
3. How humans arrived.
4. Whether any people now on the island descends from the Builders.
5. What the machines are for.
6. Whether there is anything beyond the sea.

**Six, and only six.** Everything else about the past can be settled by a proposal. If a buried truth is ever wanted for a late reveal, it will be one truth, chosen then, by the project, and it will not contradict anything shipped.

## 9. Voice

- **No chosen one.** The player's squad is some people. Nobody has a destiny; some people have a plan.
- **Consequence over lethality.** People go down, lose limbs, come back changed. A bad day costs an arm, a friend or a town's goodwill, not a game over.
- **People are practical.** Nobody makes speeches. A captain wants your rifle, a factor wants your debt, an elder wants you gone before dark.
- **Humour is dry, situational, and never winks.** A town called "Second Attempt". A prosthetic hand with a previous owner's initials. A machine that has been repainting the same wall for eighty years. The situation is the joke; no character tells it.
- **Function first.** A description says what a thing does before how it feels.

**Item descriptions** are two sentences: what it is, then one concrete detail with a history in it. *Laser musket. A Builder cell in a hand-built housing, cranked to a charge, one shot. Someone died getting the cell, and it was probably not the person selling it.* **Faction and town blurbs**: three or four sentences, what they run, want and fear, no history older than the founding. **NPC lines and notices**: short, practical, regional; a Guild notice reads like a bill. **Research rows** name the technique, not the discovery: "Air reservoirs", never "The pneumatic revolution".

**Never**: exclamation marks in system text; "ancient", "legendary", "mysterious" as descriptors of Builder things (describe what is seen); lore dumps; a narrator; a canonical account of the past; a faction that is evil; a character who jokes.

**Found text** (journals, notices, letters) points at what the world actually does. The witness is usually the content: a machine on a hill is generated, a man obsessed with one is written. The failure that matters is describing something the game does not do.

## 10. Starts

The game gives a situation, not a reason: a trader with a cart and debts, an explorer with a map full of gaps, homesteaders with tools and no money, a deserter with a militia weapon and people looking for it, and, once the peoples exist, a start for each of them. Each is a loadout, a reputation table and a spawn region. None is a story.

## 11. How to propose

A proposal is a short document: who the people are, what they want, what they are good at, what their things look like from thirty metres, how they treat humans, the machines and each other, and one or two situations a player would meet them in. Where it touches a mystery, it offers a belief, not a fact. Where it touches the aesthetic, it cites the art sheet. It goes into the relevant bible file's Open list, or into a plate, never quietly into a data row. The project picks; what is picked becomes decided, and the next sheet says so.
