# Lore direction sheet

2026-09-15. The one page handed to anyone who writes, draws or designs for this world and needs to know what it is, what is decided, and what must stay unexplained. Condensed from `00-setting-and-history.md`, `01-peoples-and-factions.md`, `02-technology.md`, `03-the-island.md`, `04-tone-sheet.md` and `06-the-machines.md`. The companion is `2026-09-15-concept-art-direction-sheet.md`, which covers how it looks; this one covers what it is. Names in `[brackets]` are placeholders; the island and the game have no name yet.

---

## 1. The world in a paragraph

An island, somewhere, about 100 km across, desert on the coast and dry terraced mountains inside. Long ago a civilisation built cities on the ridges and paved roads across all of it, and then stopped. Nobody alive knows who they were or what ended them. Their machines still walk the ruins doing jobs nobody understands. A few generations back, humans arrived and do not remember how. They settled the coast, built towns of cast stone and riveted iron along the old roads, and now the coast is full. Uphill lives an older people who never left and say the ruins are theirs. The game starts at the point where the powers on the island have begun to press on each other. It does not tell the player which side to take, or whether to take one.

## 2. The tone in one line

**The world does not care about you, and it is not trying to kill you either.** Kenshi's indifference, Fallout's readable rubble, a frontier's practicality. Things are hard because they are hard, not because a villain made them so.

## 3. Four layers of time

The further back, the less anyone knows. The game never gives a clean account of anything older than the founding of the towns.

1. **The Builders.** Everything about them is inference from what they left: stone cut so precisely a knife will not go between the blocks, terraces up whole mountainsides, a paved road network with way-stations a day's walk apart that outlived everything else, machines still working, no writing anyone can read, and a scale that almost fits: doorways a little too tall, stairs a little too steep, statues that look human from a distance and less so up close. Settlers say "the Builders" or "them". The Uplanders have their own name and do not translate it.
2. **The Silence.** Whatever ended them. The ruins carry no wound: no burned cities, no mass graves. Abandoned, not destroyed.
3. **The Arrival.** Humans came a few generations ago and their own accounts contradict each other: a shipwreck on the western reefs, a door in the highlands that closed behind them, waking on a beach with no memory, descent from Builders who fell so far they forgot. The founding generation is dead, nobody has a document from before, there is no known home and no serious person talks about leaving. The Driftfolk arrived the same way at another time and remember as little.
4. **The Founding and now.** Towns on the coast, on the Builder roads. Trade grew a guild, fear grew militias, the militias grew into something like a law. For three or four generations the coast was enough. Now the good land is taken, the towns look uphill, the first surveys have gone up the old roads, and some came back.

## 4. Who is on the island

| Bloc | Who | Where | What they want |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Coastal settlers** (humans) | A few generations old, no capital, no king; power is whoever runs a town | Coast and lowlands | Land, trade, order on their own terms |
| **`[Uplanders]`** (alien species one) | Old, organised, tall and narrow; institutions, a scholar class, a law | Highlands, in and around the ruins | To be left alone, and to keep the ruins |
| **`[Driftfolk]`** (alien species two) | Stranded like the humans, fewer, no territory; crews and families | Scattered through everyone else's towns and the land nobody wants | A place, any place |
| **The machines** | Drones and mechs, Builder leftovers | The ruins, and the dead zones | Unknown. They act like they have a job |
| **Outlaws and nomads** | Anyone | The roads between | Money, freedom, or not being in a town |

Three institutions cross town lines among the settlers:

- **The `[Guild]`**: the merchants. Runs the caravans, sets the prices, lends the money, owns a piece of every town. Not an army, but it pays for several. Wants the roads open and the uphill push slow and profitable.
- **The `[Militias]`**: one per town of size. Watchmen who became the closest thing to law. Some honest, some the mayor's private army; militias from different towns do not automatically get on. Want the uphill push now, and to be the ones doing it.
- **The `[Technicians]`**: the youngest. The people who stopped treating Builder tech as loot and started taking it apart, and now build the coast's only high tech: cell generators, energy weapons of their own design, optics, radio, powered limbs and armour, the first engines. They sell to both sides of every quarrel and are the one power the Guild cannot buy and the militias cannot take, because the goods die with the people who maintain them. Nobody wants to march uphill without their machines, which is why the war has not started yet.

**No faction is good.** The Guild is useful and predatory. The militias keep order and start wars. The Uplanders are dignified and will leave you to die on their road. The Driftfolk are sympathetic and will rob you. The Technicians are indispensable and know it.

## 5. The present balance

There is no single war. There are several powers who would fight given a reason, and the reasons are accumulating: the coastal towns pushing uphill against the Uplanders (the war the player sees first, the front line on the map); the towns against each other; everyone courting the Technicians; everyone quietly against the machines, until a survey party goes somewhere it should not; outlaws against all of the above. The player's actions tip it. The world has a state, the state drifts, and a squad with enough force or enough money changes it.

**The uphill push is a land grab** by people who need land against people who were there first. The game shows it plainly and takes no side. Uplanders are never primitive; settlers are never heroes for going uphill.

## 6. Technology: the ladder

Slow, handmade, mechanical. The coast builds precise machines one at a time by hand and cannot mass-produce anything. Every machine shows how it works.

| Tier | Name | Who | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Hand craft | Anyone with a bench | Knives, bows, iron swords, leather, palisades, campfires |
| 1 | Frontier machines | Towns with a smithy and a millwright | Steam generators, wind turbines, mills, pneumatic rifles, iron plate armour, stone walls, cables and lamps |
| 2 | Master work and relics | A handful of craftsmen; the ruins | Laser muskets built around a salvaged Builder cell, repeater rifles (a few dozen on the island), precision optics, salvage limbs |
| 3 | Settler high tech | The Technicians, and a player who has done the work | Cell generators, coil rifles and arc guns, sensors, radio, powered prosthetics, powered armour, the first engines on wheels |
| 4 | Builder tech | Nobody | Drones, mechs, whatever powers the ruins. Salvage only |

**The player can build everything but tier 4**, at a cost: tier 3 needs time, a workshop, salvage from the dead zones and a Technician's blueprint or a Technician to start from. A fast weapon is an event. When one appears, the world should notice.

The Uplanders' ladder is different, not worse: textiles, ceramics, stone and bronze worked to a standard humans cannot match, and a few things nobody can explain.

## 7. The island

Think of the Andes compressed onto an island and take it literally: **the coast is desert.** Dry scrub, salt flats, dune, a river mouth every few days' walk where the only farmland is. Green is what people make with water, never what the land gives. **Water is the resource**: a well is a claim, a cistern is a town. Timber is scarce and is wealth.

- **Coast and lowlands**: the settlers. Towns at river mouths and Builder cisterns, fishing, the Guild's harbours, irrigated wheat, homesteads up every valley with water. Nothing here wants to kill you on sight except a bandit with a grudge.
- **Foothills**: the frontier. Half-built settler towns, Uplander sites recently abandoned or recently fortified, Builder way-stations held by whoever got there first, the good iron and stone. The most volatile zone.
- **Highlands**: Uplander country. Terraced valleys, stone towns, ruins still lived in, and the great Builder cities on the ridges that even the Uplanders do not enter. A human who behaves can trade here. One with a militia escort cannot.
- **Dead zones**: sites, not a region, where the machines are dense and hostile and the ruins richest. Nobody lives in one. A destination, not a wall.
- **The sea**: the one thing that lives without being made to. Fishing near the coast; boats that go too far do not return, or return with nothing.

**The roads are the Builders'.** Paved, graded, bridged, stepped, and indifferent to the borders drawn since. A road runs from a Guild harbour straight through the front line into an Uplander market and on to a dead zone. Every town sits where a road happened to be. Roads people built since are dirt tracks.

The economic engines: the **iron gradient** (poor coastal iron, the good seams in the foothills) drives the frontier war; the **water gradient** drives everything else.

## 8. The machines

Behaviour only, never explanation. A player watching machines for an hour can describe what they do and cannot say why.

- **They have jobs.** Carrying, cutting, sorting a pile into two piles, repainting a wall, walking a route. Idle the way a tool is idle, not the way an animal is.
- **They are not one thing.** Different machines behave differently enough that a player will assume kinds, sides or territories. The game never confirms which.
- **They fight each other**, in certain places, repeatedly, without conclusion. The wreckage is the richest salvage and the most dangerous ground.
- **They collect worked metal.** A settlement that hoards enough of it in one place starts attracting them. Nobody in the world knows more than *they come for metal*, and the game never states the rule; the player learns it by being raided.
- **They can be walked past.** Most machines ignore most people most of the time. The Uplanders are better at it and will not say how.
- **Scale is human.** A machine may be a landmark. No machine is a region, an army or an endgame, and nothing visibly makes new machines.

## 9. What the game never says

Six load-bearing mysteries. No text, data row, faction blurb or NPC line resolves them; every in-game source that touches them disagrees with some other source, and each account must read as a plausible distortion of the same real thing, not an invention.

1. What the Builders were: human, not human, something that made humans. All three are believed by somebody.
2. What ended them.
3. How humans arrived.
4. Whether the Uplanders are the Builders' descendants. They say so. Their tech does not obviously derive from Builder tech, and their bodies do not obviously fit Builder doorways either.
5. What the machines are for.
6. Whether there is anything beyond the sea.

**Six, and only six.** Everything else about the past can be settled. If a buried truth is ever wanted for a late reveal, it will be one truth, chosen then, and it will not contradict anything shipped. No contributor picks it.

## 10. Voice

- **No chosen one.** The player's squad is some people. Nobody has a destiny; some people have a plan.
- **Consequence over lethality.** People go down, lose limbs, come back changed. A bad day costs an arm, a friend or a town's goodwill, not a game over.
- **People are practical.** Nobody makes speeches. A militia captain wants your rifle, a Guild factor wants your debt, an Uplander elder wants you gone before dark.
- **Humour is dry, situational, and never winks.** A town called "Second Attempt". A prosthetic hand with a previous owner's initials. A machine that has been repainting the same wall for eighty years. The situation is the joke; no character tells it.
- **Scale is human.** The biggest thing on the island is a town of a few hundred and a militia of fifty. A raid of twenty is serious; a fight over a fortified town tops out around forty. No empires, no capital.
- **Function first.** A description says what a thing does before how it feels.

**Item descriptions** are two sentences: what it is, then one concrete detail with a history in it. *Laser musket. A Builder cell in a hand-built housing, cranked to a charge, one shot. Someone died getting the cell, and it was probably not the person selling it.* **Faction and town blurbs**: three or four sentences, what they run, want and fear, no history older than the founding. **NPC lines and notices**: short, practical, regional; a Guild notice reads like a bill, an Uplander line is courteous and gives nothing away. **Research rows** name the technique, not the discovery: "Air reservoirs", never "The pneumatic revolution".

**Never**: exclamation marks in system text; "ancient", "legendary", "mysterious" as descriptors of Builder things (describe what is seen); lore dumps; a narrator; a canonical account of the past; a faction that is evil; a character who jokes.

**Found text** (journals, notices, letters) points at what the world actually does. It can certify time the player never spends ("it has done the same thing every day for a week"), and the witness is usually the content: a machine on a hill is generated, a man obsessed with one is written. Where it is found is half of it. The failure that matters is describing something the game does not do.

## 11. Starts

The game gives a situation, not a reason: a **trader** (cart, coins, goods, a mule, a Guild letter, debts), an **explorer** (a map with gaps, a rifle, a reason to go uphill), **homesteaders** (tools, seed, no money, unclaimed land), a **deserter** (one unit, a militia weapon, people looking for it), and later an Uplander exile, a Driftfolk crew, an outlaw band. Each is a loadout, a reputation table and a spawn region. None is a story.

## 12. What a contributor decides

Anything on the bible's *Open* lists is open to proposal. The ones most likely to matter to a writer or designer:

- Real names for the five blocs, both alien species, the island and the game.
- The Driftfolk's distinctive competence and their look.
- Each alien species' competence in the skills table.
- How long ago the Arrival was, in years.
- Whether a far shore exists and whether anything ever arrives from it.
- The island's animals, by zone; climate and seasons.
- Whether the dead zones have bad air, making a mask with filters an expedition requirement.
- Whether machines ever take a side in the uphill war.
- The Technicians in detail: their halls, their politics, what they refuse to sell.

A proposal goes into the relevant file's Open list or a plate, never quietly into a data row. Everything not listed as open is decided for now.
